# Marketing & Growth Brief — DJ (Apple Music)

**To:** Apple Music Marketing & Growth
**From:** Dylan Ibrahimi, Product
**Date:** September 10, 2026
**Re:** Positioning, naming, and Phase 1 discovery

---

## The problem you're being handed

We are shipping an autonomous, self-narrating listening session. Spotify shipped a feature that sounds like this in February 2023. **The first sentence written about us will be "Apple copied Spotify's DJ, three years late," unless we give people a better sentence first.**

That's the assignment. Not awareness — differentiation, against a comparison that is already loaded and waiting.

## The counter-position

The two products are not the same thing, and the difference is defensible.

Spotify's DJ is a **synthetic host**: a generated voice, a persona, conversational banter between songs. Ours has no host at all. Every piece of context our DJ shows is **sourced from material Apple's editors actually wrote** — editor notes, artist and album facts, release context, Apple Music 1 segments. When we have nothing real to say about a track, we say nothing.

That isn't a limitation we're spinning. It's the thing Spotify structurally cannot copy, because they don't run an editorial operation. Apple Music's differentiator has been human curation since launch — Apple Music 1, real hosts, real editors. This feature is that operation, sequenced into a session that builds itself.

**The line to land:** the sessions build themselves; the words are still written by people.

## Naming recommendation — and a strong one

**Do not lead with "AI."**

Every use of "AI DJ" in our own materials does Spotify's comparison work for us. It invites the reader to line the two features up side by side, on the axis where Spotify has three years of head start and a more attention-grabbing gimmick. Worse, it frames a feature whose whole selling point is *human-written context* as a machine talking — which is precisely backwards, and hands critics the "Apple's AI host is worse than Spotify's" story we have no way to win.

The product's internal working name can stay whatever engineering finds useful. In market: name the experience, not the technology. We should be comfortable saying how it works when asked — the sourcing story is a strength — but the technology should never be the headline.

I expect pushback on this, and it's worth the argument. Ceding the AI framing is a decision we can't easily reverse once the first press cycle has run.

## What not to say

- Don't claim it "knows you better than you know yourself." It's a session builder, not a mind reader, and the claim invites a specific kind of ridicule.
- Don't promise a personality. We deliberately don't have one, and users who arrive expecting banter will bounce.
- Don't compare directly to Spotify in owned channels. Naming them makes the comparison our frame.

## Growth: the actual Phase 1 problem is reach, not persuasion

Our primary success metric is stickiness among people who've tried it. That number is meaningless if only a sliver of users ever find the feature — a great stickiness figure over a tiny base is not a win, and we'd have no way to tell the difference.

So Phase 1's growth job is **discovery**, and we're tracking trial rate as a funnel metric: the share of exposed monthly actives who start at least one session within 30 days. **The 15% floor we're working to is an assumption, not a benchmark** — nobody has published adoption numbers for a feature like this. Treat it as a starting hypothesis to be replaced by real data at week 2, not a target to be hit.

Two constraints on how we pursue it:

- In-product placement carries this — Home hero and sidebar. The feature has to be found where people already are.
- **No push-notification campaign.** A feature whose pitch is restraint should not be marketed by interrupting people.

## The audience that matters most

The strategic target isn't everyone — it's **discovery under-engagers**: subscribers who mostly replay what they already know and rarely touch algorithmic recommendations. Phase 1 measures this segment separately, and messaging built for people who already love recommendations will miss them entirely. Speaking to someone who thinks "I already know what I want to hear" is a harder and more specific job than a generic launch.

## What we need back

1. A positioning line and message hierarchy built on the sourced-editorial difference.
2. A naming recommendation, with a view on the "AI" question above.
3. An in-product discovery plan for the Phase 1 beta market.
